## Motivation

The main reason I wanted to go on exchange during my undergrad was to experience life abroad before heading overseas for a master's or PhD, and at the same time work on projects there and get to know professors and researchers — with future recommendation letters for grad school applications in mind. Of course, traveling around Europe and soaking in the academic atmosphere at ETH, the world's #4 school for CS, were huge draws too. Being Einstein's junior schoolmate for half a year is pretty cool, after all.

## Zürich

Zürich is Switzerland's largest city, yet it only has about 400,000 residents — noticeably free of the suffocating crowds you sometimes get in Taiwan. Despite the low population density, public transit is excellent: trains, trams, trolleybuses, and buses weave Zürich and its surrounding towns into a dense network. I once read that nowhere in the city center is more than a 15-minute walk from a stop. Even in the quieter suburbs, buses come every 15 minutes. Blue-and-white trams threading through the city's narrow streets is, to me, the most iconic sight in Zürich.

I remember during orientation everyone kept asking each other what they thought of Zürich. My answer was always: "The scenery is beautiful, and other than all the people smoking on the street, I can't think of a single downside" (things being expensive is not Switzerland's fault).

Maybe it's because there's a lake right in the city, but the air in Zürich always felt fresh. On sunny days, the blue lake glitters gold in the light against a backdrop of mountains, and sometimes I'd catch myself thinking how lucky I was to live here.

{{<figure src="/image/zurich-lake.jpeg" title="Lake Zürich in the sunshine" >}}

Once winter arrives, though, the weather turns gloomy. The lake makes Zürich prone to overcast skies, and whenever the sun disappears behind the clouds the whole city fades into gray. Combined with the short daylight hours, it can get pretty depressing.

## The Dorm

Our dorm was in Zollikon, southeast of Zürich — a wealthy part of the canton, quiet and scenic, where the viewing deck of a nearby high school looks straight out over Lake Zürich. It's less than a 15-minute drive from the city center, but because it's technically a different municipality, every transit ride costs an extra zone. Things were already expensive; paying extra on top really stung.

Not being in the same zone as ETH is a real downside, but honestly it might be the only one (being a bit pricey isn't the dorm's flaw — it's mine). It's close to the bus stop and to both Migros and Coop (Switzerland's two biggest supermarkets), so if you forget an ingredient mid-cooking you can just pop across the street and be back in minutes. The rooms are huge, the shared kitchen is huge, my room happened to have a great view, and the floor heating was set to a toasty constant 25°C — snow falling outside while you lounge in shorts and a t-shirt inside, sometimes even sweating in your sleep. I didn't realize how unusual this was until I visited other dorms and found theirs fixed at 20°C, still cold at night. Another perk is that most residents are exchange students, so "in theory" it's easier to make friends — the local students are genuinely busy. That huge kitchen and common room make the place a paradise for extroverts: whenever you feel like chatting, you can usually find someone around.

## Cooking

Before the exchange I had never cooked a single meal. I loved watching cooking videos — Wang Gang, James — but had never actually cooked, so before leaving I was genuinely worried I'd starve. I kept pestering my mom to let me practice, and she just said, "You'll figure it out once you're abroad." She was right.

Maybe all those cooking videos rubbed off on me, because cooking turned out to be not that hard: read a recipe, trust your instincts, and it comes out pretty tasty. Eating out there is absurdly expensive — even the cheapest campus canteen meal averages 7–8 Swiss francs, and trekking to campus just to eat felt silly. Groceries aren't cheap either, but cooking is still far cheaper, and you get to make the dishes you actually love. The downside is time: with prep and washing up, a meal easily takes an hour — a world away from Taiwan, where you can hit a canteen or order delivery. But eating something delicious that you made yourself is deeply satisfying. Maybe it's just because I love food, but I'd say cooking was one of the most fun and rewarding parts of my exchange (even better when the kitchen is empty).

## Fun

Entertainment options aren't exactly abundant. Beyond some bars and clubs, everything else is remarkably wholesome — the most popular pastime being hiking.
Zürich sits on hilly terrain, and even as Switzerland's largest city it has plenty of untouched forest on its outskirts. From Zollikon, a ten-minute walk got me to a decent-sized forest with well-maintained trails. Whenever I felt down, a short walk there fixed most of it. Besides the hills, Lake Zürich was my other go-to spot for aimless strolls — also ten minutes away, down a fairly steep slope from the dorm. At the lake you can dip your hands in the water, or actually swim in summer. Looking out at the mountains across the water, catching a sunset that the clouds spared, I'd sometimes just feel incredibly lucky to be on exchange somewhere this beautiful.

{{<figure src="/image/uetliberg.jpeg" title="Uetliberg, on the outskirts of Zürich" >}}

ETH students can also sign up for ASVZ classes and use their gyms for free. ASVZ is a bit like a public sports center, but with courses for basically every sport you can think of — even baseball, and skiing in winter! I took a badminton class at the start of the semester, and it was intense: the coach seriously drilled our core strength, and I was wrecked after every session (sadly, after two classes ASVZ scheduling issues canceled the course for the rest of the semester). After that I mostly played badminton with dormmates and Taiwanese friends, or took hip-hop and locking classes — the teaching quality was consistently great. Lots of friends played volleyball too and had good things to say. If you're into sports, you'll love ASVZ.

## Courses

I took three courses: Visual Computing, Computer Vision, and Computer Graphics. Visual Computing is an undergraduate course; the other two are graduate courses. Many lower-year undergrad courses are taught in German, so without German they're basically off the table. I arrived in Switzerland too late for the intensive pre-semester German course, and I skipped the during-semester one because I was worried about being too busy to travel — which I now regret, since German class is a great way to meet people, and I later discovered German is actually fun to learn. So: take the German course!

One great thing here is that the bigger courses record every lecture, so you don't actually have to show up. Once the winter rains set in, I mostly stayed in the dorm catching up on lecture videos.

The exam system deserves a mention because it's bizarre. There are two kinds of finals: end-of-semester exams and session exams. An end-of-semester exam works like finals in Taiwan — held in the last week or two before the break. Session exams are different: the school first sends you off on vacation, and then, before the next semester starts, runs a month-long exam session, with your exams randomly scheduled anywhere in that month depending on overall enrollment. In my semester, the break started before Christmas (Dec 23) and ran until Jan 19, and session exams stretched from Jan 20 to Feb 14 — brutal. Each course's session exam date isn't announced until the end of the semester, so until then I had no idea when I'd finish all three exams and could fly back to Taiwan, which also meant I couldn't book flights. Lunar New Year 2025 fell early, so if my session exams hadn't finished in the first week, I'd have spent New Year in Zürich QQ. In the end, Computer Vision and Computer Graphics were in the first week of the session, but Visual Computing dragged on into the third week, so I got back to Taiwan just before NYCU's semester started — my first Lunar New Year abroad. If you're worried about this, go in the spring semester instead; at least you won't be studying for exams while freezing and miserable. By the way, the GPA here tops out at 6.0, with grades in steps of 0.5 if I remember correctly.


### Visual Computing

This course is essentially an intro to Computer Vision + Computer Graphics: CV in the first half of the semester, CG in the second. The teaching lineup is stacked — Marc Pollefeys teaches the CV half and Markus Gross the CG half. Marc was the PhD advisor of COLMAP's author (COLMAP being arguably the strongest and most famous optimization-based SfM tool today) and has advised many important 3D reconstruction works; you could call him the GOAT of SfM. Markus founded Disney Research Zurich, has published countless SIGGRAPH papers, and the technology behind many famous Disney films traces directly back to him and his lab.

There are no assignments and no quizzes — your grade is decided entirely by the session exam. On top of that, I assumed my other two courses covered everything in this one (turns out the overlap is tiny), so honestly I barely looked at it during the semester. Still, as an introduction to visual computing it's quite good — you get a tour of the important concepts and algorithms in both CV and CG.

Truth be told, only when I started studying for the final did I realize how heavy this course is. Being a survey course, it covers a LOT: the CV half races through camera models, detection, segmentation, classification, and 3D reconstruction, while the CG half covers rasterization algorithms plus all sorts of odd little topics like Bézier curves. Tons of knowledge points, each touched on only briefly, without much detail or underlying theory — so a lot of it comes down to memorization, which made studying painful. You can bring a cheat sheet, but the material is so scattered that it barely helps. Only after working through several past exams did I finally get a feel for the course. Overall it's an everything-included survey; if there's something else you'd rather take, I wouldn't say this one is a must. The semester itself is easy — the exam prep is what hurts.

### Computer Vision

Computer Vision is co-taught by Marc Pollefeys, Siyu Tang, and one of Siyu's post-docs, each covering a third. The content overlaps quite a bit with Visual Computing, mostly going deeper. The course consists of five assignments plus a session exam. The assignments aren't hard — about three to four hours each for me (with help from LLMs) — and the exam isn't too bad either, which of course means you need a very high exam score for a good final grade. I failed to complete one proof during the exam and ended up with a 5.5.

The teaching quality is decent and the slides are readable — apart from Marc's SfM lectures, which drown you in mathematical notation — everything was easy to absorb. The assignments, while not difficult, do a complete job of exercising the concepts. All in all, a solid, by-the-book CV course.

### Computer Graphics

Computer Graphics is taught mainly by Markus's students and graduated PhDs — Markus himself was so busy that I think he only showed up for three or four lectures all semester? None of which stops this from being the best course I've ever taken.

It has essentially zero overlap with the CG half of Visual Computing. The focus here is physically-based rendering. The core question of CG is: "How do I use a computer to produce ultra-realistic images?" Physically-based rendering answers it by simulating real-world physics — refraction, reflection, diffuse scattering, and so on. Different materials reflect light differently: metals barely scatter diffusely, with most light obeying the law of reflection at specific angles, while plastics scatter light in every direction across the hemisphere. Beyond solids, simulating how light bounces through clouds, fog, and smoke is another central problem in CG. The course teaches all of the above — and then, since truly simulating real-world light would require sampling infinitely many rays from the camera, each potentially splitting exponentially through scattering, it also teaches how to render complex scenes accurately and efficiently.

Even with something like six different people lecturing over the semester, the course is so well organized that everyone used the same slide template, and every topic was taught by the person who specializes in it — so every single lecture was excellent, with figures and formulas that were easy to follow and a consistent style throughout. Beautifully done teaching. Beyond the lectures, the assignments and final project are also superbly designed, all revolving around one theme: a "physically-based renderer".

One of Markus's students — now a professor at EPFL, I believe — wrote an educational physically-based renderer called Nori. It comes with the essential data structures already defined, like a BVH, plus a threading library so the renderer can use multiple threads. Each assignment builds on the previous one, adding new features to make the renderer more complete. The assignments aren't easy, but the TAs are wonderful — they know the assignments inside out and will happily debug alongside you on GitLab or at in-person TA sessions. Every assignment ships with its tests, and there are no hidden tests: pass the tests, get the points, no anxiety about surprise failures. The final project extends the renderer with advanced features chosen from a long list, each worth points by difficulty — 30, 20, or 5 — and you pick features summing to 60 points, including at least one 30-point feature. The hardest features, like the smoke and clouds that need volume rendering, are worth the most. You can work solo or in pairs; even in a pair, each person must implement 60 points' worth of features (and you can't pick the same ones as your teammate), so there's none of the free-riding you get in other courses' group projects. And because Nori is so well modularized, merging two people's features produces one gloriously feature-rich renderer. The course tradition is a rendering competition at the end of the semester: each team designs a scene matching the year's theme (ours was "In Between") and renders it with their own renderer. In the final week, the TAs invite researchers and animators from Disney to judge every team's image and features, and the winning team gets a free trip to EUROGRAPHICS, the European graphics conference. So cool!

We made it to the finals and presented our render to the judges, but I guess it wasn't quite awesome enough (maybe not on-theme enough), so we didn't win — still an amazing experience.

{{<figure src="/image/cg_render.jpg" title="Our final render!" >}}

This is the best and most solid course I've taken so far. Besides the assignments and final project, there's also a session exam — a genuinely hard one. I don't think anyone can finish it: nearly 30 pages in 100 minutes is just not happening. The TAs said as much — don't try to finish, just pick the problems you know. I actually like that exam philosophy, and the exam never felt like it was trying to trick you; working through the CG exam was almost enjoyable. Doing past papers, I got to re-derive a lot of the math from lecture: Monte Carlo sampling, BSDFs, volume rendering, and all sorts of magical warping.

Physically-based rendering may look like it's fading lately with the rise of data-driven diffusion models like RGB↔X and DiffusionRenderer, but I think it's still absolutely worth understanding how the Disney films we grew up with evolved from Toy Story to Moana — fluids, hair, and every kind of exotic particle effect included. By the way, one of the TAs worked at Disney for 30 years and is now on leave to do a PhD, so you get to hear firsthand how the old Disney films were made — how a movie goes from script to character design, storyboards, modeling, rendering, and beyond. This course is fantastic. If you're doing a fall exchange, take it. It's demanding (though manageable during the semester), and you'll come out with a remarkably complete understanding of computer graphics.

## Traveling

Enough about Zürich — let's talk about everything outside it. Transport is superb: the enormous Hauptbahnhof (central station) has 50-something platforms above and below ground, with direct trains to Lucerne, Geneva, Lausanne, Bern, Strasbourg, Milan, Munich, and Vienna. Even Zermatt, the ski resort town, is a day-trip distance away. My girlfriend and I went to Zermatt to see the Matterhorn right before leaving Zürich — day-tripping it saved us a night's lodging. With enough time and planning you can hit Switzerland's big sights — Jungfrau, Pilatus, Titlis — plus ski trips and the Glacier Express. Switzerland too expensive? Take the direct train through the tunnel under the Alps to Milan next door: shopping, luxury goods, the Duomo, and delicious, affordable Italian food (seriously good!). In October there's Oktoberfest in Munich. That's the beauty of being based in Zürich — everywhere is easy to reach. I even flew to Iceland mid-semester for almost two weeks; a round trip on easyJet with no checked luggage cost only ~6,000 TWD. Ridiculously cheap. Coming to Europe on exchange means traveling Europe to bits — though I was often tired or sick, or maybe just bad at enjoying life, and sometimes spent weekends vegetating in the dorm, which I now slightly regret. My advice: before your exchange, pick the places you're dying to visit and let that excitement carry you. But don't let fear of missing out consume you either — traveling comfortably and happily matters more.

## Closing Thoughts

The half year in Switzerland was one of the most special experiences of my life. It gave me so much that I could never have found inside my comfort zone in Taiwan: a long-distance relationship, language barriers, culture shock, independence, and learning to face and handle whatever life throws at you. I grew a lot in those five months, met people from all over the world, and came away knowing myself — and my future direction — much better. Honestly, half a year isn't quite enough; just as I was settling in, it was time to leave. The perfect length is probably a year, but I'd have gone bankrupt first.
